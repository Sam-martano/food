Fats

Dietary Fat Subtypes in the U.S. Diet (1999–2016)

Dietary fats are classified chemically by the number of double bonds in their fatty acid chains:

  • Saturated Fatty Acids (SFAs): No double bonds. Found primarily in animal products (beef, pork, dairy, butter) and tropical oils (palm, coconut). Public health guidelines recommend limiting SFAs to under 10% of total daily calories.
  • Monounsaturated Fatty Acids (MUFAs): One double bond. Found in olive oil, avocados, canola oil, and many nuts. Often considered cardio-protective.
  • Polyunsaturated Fatty Acids (PUFAs): Two or more double bonds, including essential omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids. Found in plant and seed oils (soybean, sunflower, corn), walnuts, flaxseed, and fatty fish.
  • Trans Fatty Acids (Trans Fats): Created industrially by partially hydrogenating liquid vegetable oils to make them solid at room temperature. Heavily linked to systemic inflammation and cardiovascular disease.

Saturated fats remain solid at room temperature and have historically been targeted by nutritional guidance due to their effect on raising LDL (“bad”) cholesterol.

  • Primary Sources: Fatty cuts of beef and pork, cheese, whole milk, butter, ice cream, cured meats (sausage, bacon), and palm oil in processed snacks.
  • Health Role: While necessary in moderate amounts for cellular structure and hormone production, consumption above 10% of total energy is associated with increased coronary heart disease risk. American intake has hovered around 11.5%–11.9% without declining.

Monounsaturated fats are typically liquid at room temperature and semi-solid when refrigerated. They are widely considered heart-healthy.

  • Primary Sources: Olive oil, canola oil, avocados, almonds, peanuts, cashews, and peanut butter.
  • Health Role: MUFAs help lower LDL cholesterol while maintaining protective HDL cholesterol levels. Intake has remained very stable at ~13% of daily calories.

Polyunsaturated fats include essential fatty acids that the human body cannot produce on its own: omega-3s and omega-6s.

  • Primary Sources: Soybean oil, corn oil, sunflower oil, walnuts, chia seeds, flaxseed, and fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines).
  • Health Role: PUFAs support brain function, cell membrane fluidity, and reduce cardiovascular events when substituting for saturated fat. Their share of energy intake climbed from 7.6% to 8.2%, largely driven by the growth of vegetable oils in prepared and restaurant foods.

Industrial trans fats are formed when hydrogen is bubbled through vegetable oil (partial hydrogenation) to create a cheap, shelf-stable solid fat.

  • Historical Sources: Commercial margarine, vegetable shortening, deep-fried fast food, microwave popcorn, and packaged baked goods (cookies, frostings, pies).
  • Health Role: Trans fats are considered the most hazardous dietary fat because they simultaneously raise LDL cholesterol and lower HDL cholesterol while promoting systemic vascular inflammation. Intake plummeted from 2.6% down to 0.5% following the FDA’s mandatory labeling rule in 2006 and the subsequent ban on partially hydrogenated oils.

Key Takeaways

  1. A Major Public Health Victory: The near-elimination of industrial trans fat from the food supply demonstrates that regulatory and labeling policies can effectively transform the American diet.
  2. Persistent Saturated Fat Surplus: Despite decades of dietary guidelines recommending that saturated fat intake stay below 10% of calories, adult consumption remains at ~11.9%.
  3. The Rise of Seed and Vegetable Oils: Polyunsaturated fat intake has steadily expanded, reflecting the widespread presence of commercial soybean and vegetable oils in modern processed foods.
